# On the Delight of the Will, or the History of Its Seizing, Its Opening, and Its Release

## Prolegomenon

It is not given to everyone to follow delight back to where it begins, and it is given to few to hold fast to the whole path from the first craving to a delight that no longer craves. Most would rather have it said in a single sentence what delight is, and two such sentences lie ready. The first is old and has the authority of common experience: delight is the fulfilment of a desire. The second is bolder, and has lately been put forward with the confidence of a discovery: that *all* delight of the will arises from the exploring and handling of structures.

Neither is to be dismissed. The first has on its side everyone who has hungered and eaten, sought and found; it knows that delight follows upon fulfilment, and knows it more surely than any argument could teach. The second sees further. It has noticed that the will does not delight in an empty satisfaction but in something articulated, something that can be followed and held, and that we delight most in what we can explore and in what we can master. Far from being idle opinions, both are, as will be shown, the necessary appearance of delight at a determinate point of its history. Each says truly what delight is *there*.

But a proposition that is true in its place becomes false when it is made to hold everywhere, and then it ends by saying nothing. Whoever wishes to secure the “all” by widening the will until it includes every inclination, and structure until it includes every experience, will certainly succeed. But he will have purchased the universality of the thesis with its content. A proposition that excludes nothing affirms nothing; it is a night in which every delight looks alike. We shall therefore neither grant these propositions at the outset nor refute them. We shall arrive at them.

Nor shall we define delight, as one gives the meaning of a foreign word. We shall follow it. What follows is a history: of a will that first seizes and opens, that is then driven round between its seizing and its opening, and that only at the last, and only by a decision it cannot compel, comes to delight. Each stage has its truth only in its place, and each of the two propositions will be found in its own place before it is left behind. Whoever takes the first stage for the last has not understood the first.

It will be seen in the course of this history that the bolder thesis was not mistaken in what it intended, but only in where it stopped. The will does delight in structure. But it delights in it neither by exploring nor by handling it. It delights in it when it has ceased to want anything of it.

Whoever would know delight must accompany the will through what it suffers before it delights.

## A) The Two Wills, or the Equal Originality of Seizing and Opening

We begin where the will shows itself most plainly: in its going out. The will strives; it goes beyond itself toward what it does not yet have. Were this the whole of the will, then delight would be nothing but the moment in which the striving reaches its object, and the first of our two propositions would already be established. It is, in fact, the first thing that can be said of delight, and the history will confirm it in its place.

Yet we want delight also to remain, to be something in which the will may dwell and not merely something through which it passes; for what is only passed through is not delighted in but got over, and relief is not delight. But striving does not of itself reach dwelling. What is reached is had, and what is had is no longer striven for. Another force, contrary to all striving, must first give the will a ground: a force that draws in, closes, and holds, by which the will gathers itself into itself and becomes capable of having anything at all.

Let us call the first the *opening* will and the second the *seizing* will. The opening will goes forth: it explores, it attends, it is drawn toward every horizon that shows itself. The seizing will draws back: it grasps, it holds fast, it makes what it meets its own. Were the will only opening, it would pour itself out across everything and possess nothing; it would be, so to speak, all eye and no hand. Were it only seizing, it would close upon itself and become nothing but its having. In the order of our exposition the opening will came first, because it is what shows itself; in the order of the thing the seizing will is first, for it must give the will a ground before anything can be opened toward. What shows itself first is not what is first.

It is not enough to see this antithesis \[*Gegensatz*\]. It must also be recognized that the two wills are equally originary, and this must be shown, not merely affirmed. Let one try to derive the seizing will from the opening. The opening will goes out; if it is to have what it reaches, it must at some point cease to go out. But ceasing is no mode of going out; it is its contrary, and what a movement negates it cannot produce. Let one try, then, the reverse. The seizing will draws in; if it is ever to grasp anything new, it must loosen its hold. But loosening is no mode of holding. In each case the derivation succeeds only by secretly presupposing what it was to derive. It is impossible that the hand which closes should have come from the hand which opens. Each has its own root.

Indeed, our age shows a marked predilection for the opening will. Discovery, curiosity, the open horizon are praised; the closed hand is thought base, and its names are appetite, possessiveness, greed. Yet the seizing will meets us everywhere and in every form: in the grasp of the infant, who knows the world first by closing upon it; in the scholar, who is not content to have understood a proof until he can *have* it, that is, reproduce it unaided; in the player, whose fingers must take a passage into themselves before it can be played at all. It is this hand that closes against the hand that opens, this breath that is held against the breath that is given, this root that holds the earth against the branch that seeks the light. Without it there would be explorers, but nothing explored would ever be kept.

Hence the will is, in accordance with its nature, both seizing and opening. Not, to be sure, as though seizing *as such* were opening; that would be a slipshod expression and would abolish the very antithesis it pretends to affirm. Rather, *that which* seizes and *that which* opens are one and the same = x, namely, the will itself. We may designate the seizing will as B and the opening will as A; the will is the x that is both.

Language, which often knows more than those who speak it, has kept this unity. The German *Lust* names at once the desire for a thing and the pleasure in it; the one who has *Lust* to do something and the one who does it *mit Lust* are, in the word, not yet distinguished. We do not build on this. It only shows, from below, what has here been shown from above.

The will that only opened would have nothing. The will that only seized would be nothing but its having.

## B) On Structure, or the Birth of Articulation from the Antithesis

It is customary to treat structure as something found ready, a lattice that lies before the will and waits to be traversed. But this only defers the question. Whence the articulation? A field without any closure would offer the hand nothing to grasp, and a field without any relation would offer the eye nothing to follow. The one would be a heap of things each shut within itself; the other a fog in which everything points to everything and nothing stands. Neither could be delighted in, for in neither is there anything to delight *in*.

Therefore articulation must be understood as the meeting of the same two forces that were found in the will. The closing force gives each member of a structure its own body: by it the member is *this* and not another, holds itself together, and resists. The opening force gives each member its reference beyond itself: by it the member points to the others, belongs to a whole, and lets itself be followed. A member is a member only insofar as it is both closed and referred. A note is a note because it has its own pitch, which cannot be bent without loss; and it is a note *in a key* because that pitch leans toward its resolution.

The old words have preserved this. *Articulus* is the small joint; the German *Gliederung* comes from *Glied*, the limb. Articulation is, in the first sense, the jointing of a body: the place where two members are held apart and held together at once. A joint that only held apart would be a fracture, and one that only held together would be a stiffness. Structure is the living joint.

Hence the structure is not merely the stage on which the will acts. It is the will’s counterpart. What the will is as x, one bearer of seizing and opening, the structure is on the side of the thing: one body that is at once closed and referred. In the closure of each member the seizing will meets what is like itself, and in the reference of each member the opening will finds what answers it. Precisely for this reason the will can be at home in a structure: it finds there the same doubling that it is.

But one must not conclude that the will has lent the structure its articulation, as the idealists of our age would have it. The closure of the thing is not borrowed from the hand. It is the thing’s own, and therefore it resists. Whoever has learned an instrument knows this: the span of the octave, the fixed relations of the keys, the weight the string demands are not posited by the player but met by him, and at first they meet him as obstacle. That which the will must first suffer, it did not make.

This resistance is not to be despised. Those who would have the will move without friction through a world entirely transparent to it want a delight without a body. Resistance is the lowest in the structure and, for that reason, its ground. Without it the opening will would find no horizon, for a horizon is only the edge of what has not yet yielded.

Where nothing resists, nothing is articulated; where nothing is articulated, nothing can be delighted in.

With this the second of our propositions has been reached, and reached not as an opinion but as a consequence. If the will is seizing and opening, and the structure is closure and reference, then the will’s commerce with structure can only be twofold. To follow the references from member to member is to *explore*; to take hold of the closure, and to bear its resistance until it yields, is to *handle*. Exploring is nothing but the opening will before a structure, and handling nothing but the seizing will before it. Whoever says that the delight of the will arises from exploring and handling has therefore seen, and seen rightly, both wills at work upon their counterpart. What he has not yet asked is what becomes of the two when each has its way.

## C) The Wheel of Obsession, or the Delight That Consumes and the Horizon That Recedes

But here, at last, the difficulty shows itself. Each of the two wills has the same claim to be that which has being \[*seyend zu seyn*\] in delight. Yet if the one is active, the other must retreat into not having being \[*nicht seyend*\], which is not nothing but mere latency; the hand cannot close and open at the same moment upon the same thing. What, then, remains to the will before a structure? Nothing but an alternation.

Let the seizing will prevail. It takes the structure into itself; it practises, masters, possesses. The passage that resisted the fingers is now in them. But at the moment of possession the structure ceases to resist, and what no longer resists no longer answers. The piece that can be played without attention is no longer heard. Mastery, which was sought as the fulfilment of delight, is its extinction. The seizing will has consumed what it wanted to hold, and from its satiety arises a peculiar emptiness, which our language calls boredom and the older language called *acedia*.

Hence the opening will breaks forth again. It turns from what it has to what it does not yet have; it seeks a new horizon, a harder piece, an unknown country. But it is the law of every horizon that it recedes as it is approached. The opening will possesses nothing; it is always on the way and never at home. Its delight is restless, and in its restlessness it begins to suffer from what it lacks. Then, from this lack, the seizing will awakens once more, and the circle begins again.

This is the wheel of obsession \[*Sucht*\]. For a craving \[*Begierde*\] that can neither rest in what it has nor cease to seek what it lacks does not fall silent; it becomes obsession. Now the will closes, now it opens; now it has and is weary, now it seeks and is homeless. The two failures which might have seemed to be two different errors, the delight that consumes its object and the striving that never arrives, are in truth the two halves of one revolution. The one is satiety without horizon, the other horizon without possession; each drives the will into the other.

The forms of this wheel are known to everyone. The collector who, no sooner has he acquired the piece he sought, is already seeking the next; the player who masters a game and lays it aside; the reader who reads only in order to have read; the lover who, possessing, grows indifferent, and, losing, burns. In all of them there is a delight, and it must not be denied. It is the hot, anxious delight of a will that does not know whether to go in or out of itself. Anxiety \[*Angst*\] is the governing affect of this stage.

Nor is this lowest delight to be despised. It is the fire without which no higher delight could be warm. Whoever has never been seized by the wheel, never craved and been weary and craved again, has not yet a will with which to delight. Yet whoever remains on the wheel delights only as one who is driven.

Here, then, both of the propositions with which we began have found their place. Delight is indeed the fulfilment of a desire, for on the wheel there is no other delight: each turn ends either in a having, which fulfils the seizing will, or in a finding, which fulfils the opening. And delight does indeed arise from the exploring and handling of structures, for these are the two wills at their work, and the wheel is nothing but their alternation. Neither proposition was false. Each was the truth of a stage taken for the truth of the whole. Now it is also clear why the first, made to hold everywhere, ends by saying nothing: fulfilment made universal is only the name for the turning of the wheel. What both describe is the delight of the driven.

How, then, is the will to be redeemed from this circling and led into a delight that does not consume itself?

## D) The Will That Wills Nothing, or Delight Proper

Not by the victory of the seizing will, for its victory is possession, and possession ends resistance. Not by the victory of the opening will, for its victory is the endless horizon, which is an empty infinite and possesses nothing. Not by a compulsion from without, for by what would the will be compelled, and to what? There is only one solution. Both must voluntarily surrender the claim to be that which has being in delight, and this they can do only before something higher, which is neither seizing nor opening.

This higher is the will that wills nothing. It is nothing, certainly; but in the way that a pure attention is nothing. It wants nothing of the structure: neither to have it nor to go beyond it. And precisely for this reason the structure may show itself whole, as it is, with its closure intact and its references unfolded. The old mystics called this releasement \[*Gelassenheit*\], and they meant by it not a weakness of will but its highest freedom.

Now, for the first time, the forces enter into their places. What was first, the seizing will, becomes the lowest, the ground of delight: it is the grip that has become the body’s own, the competence that has sunk into the hand and no longer needs to be thought. What was second, the opening will, becomes the mediator: it is the listening by which the structure is followed in its references, the attention that no longer hunts for a new horizon because it has found the depth of the present one. And what was last becomes the highest: the stillness above both, in which nothing is wanted. The “before” and “after” of the wheel become an “at once”.

From now on, therefore, the two wills are no longer to be called forces that exclude each other, but potencies that stand one above another. We designated the seizing will as B and the opening will as A. The seizing will remains B, the ground. The opening will, raised to mediator, becomes A². And the will that wills nothing, in which the x that bore both is for the first time actual as their unity, is A³.

Whoever has heard a master play knows this “at once”. The hand holds the passage, the ear opens to it, and above both there is a calm in which the piece, so to speak, plays itself. It would be false to say that the master has stopped willing. He has never willed more completely. But his will no longer wants anything of the music; it lets it be. This is delight: not the wage of the will, but the will at rest within its own movement.

Hence the distinction which the first of our propositions could not draw. Fulfilment is the satisfaction of one of the two forces; delight is the concord of both beneath the higher. A fulfilment may therefore be joyless, as the mastered piece is joyless; and a delight may come where nothing is fulfilled, as one may delight in a passage that one has not yet learned to play.

But why does this delight not consume itself in turn? Because the will that wills nothing leaves the structure its closure. It does not dissolve what in the thing is irreducible, its weight, its darkness, its refusal to be wholly taken. That which seizes would abolish this remainder; that which wills nothing preserves it. And the remainder is the guarantee that the thing is real, and therefore that it can be delighted in again. Only what keeps something back can be delighted in more than once.

Yet this release cannot be produced, and here lies the hardest point of the whole history. The wheel cannot stop itself, for every attempt to stop it is one more turn. If the seizing will resolves to renounce, its renunciation is only a seizing in reverse: it holds fast to its not-having as once it held fast to its having, and the ascetic who counts his abstentions has not left the wheel. If the opening will seeks stillness as its goal, the stillness becomes one more horizon, and recedes like every other. To will not-willing is still to will. Neither of the two can bring forth what is above both, for each could bring it forth only as one of its own acts.

What, then, does the wheel accomplish, if it cannot end itself? Nothing but its own exhaustion, and this is not little. Each turn tests anew the claim of each will to be that which has being in delight, and each turn refutes it. The seizing will learns that every possession is a loss; the opening will learns that every horizon is a deferral. Not by being taught, but by suffering its own success, each comes to the point where its claim has worn thin. This is the long labour of practice, of love, of every serious study: the will is not raised by it, but it is made ready to be raised. Delight can be prepared, but never compelled.

Then release comes as a resolution \[*Entschliessung*\], which, if one stages the word, is a dis-closing \[*Ent-Schließung*\], an opening up decisively: the closed hand opens, not because it is forced, nor because it has resolved upon opening as upon a task, but in a moment that is free and, to the one who undergoes it, like a gift. Whoever has practised an instrument for years knows this moment. One day the passage that had been fought over and possessed is no longer fought over or possessed; it is simply there, and one hears it as for the first time. No one can say on which day this happened. The decision that grounds the highest delight sinks at once beneath consciousness, and this is its mark. What we can date, we made; what we cannot date, was given.

The will that wants everything of the structure finds nothing in it. The will that wants nothing finds it whole.

## E) Objection from the Passive Pleasures, or the First and the Last Stillness

Here the objection will break forth that we have begun too late. There is, it will be said, a delight that knows nothing of seizing or opening, and still less of a wheel: the warmth of the sun on the skin, the sound of rain at night, the laughter of a child at nothing. In such delight the will neither grasps nor explores, and it has passed through no conflict. If this delight is real, it precedes our whole history, and our history is refuted at its root.

We do not answer, as others have, that such delight is secretly a striving after all, that the ear anticipates the next drop and the skin explores the warmth. This would be to derive the opposite from its opposite, and to save the thesis by the very widening of terms against which this whole investigation is directed. Certainly there is such a delight. It is real, and it is not a disguised seizing.

But what is it? It is the stillness *before* the antithesis has awakened: the will not yet divided against itself, slumbering, holding its two forces *implicite*, in an enfolded state. Its rest is genuine, but it does not know itself. It is the innocence of the beginning, and the beginning, as everywhere, is the lowest.

But how does this stillness pass into the division? Here one must be careful. A transition from contradiction to unity is natural, for contradiction is insufferable to everything; but a transition from unity into contradiction cannot be understood from the unity, for the unity lacks nothing and has no reason to leave itself. The first stillness does not divide itself. It is divided. What divides it is lack: hunger, cold, the absence of the one who was there. Lack comes to the slumbering will from its own body, which it does not yet know as its own, and finds it without defence.

In the first lack the two forces awaken together and as opposites. The will is thrown back upon itself and contracts: this is the first seizing, the grasp of the infant, which closes upon whatever is nearest. In the same moment the will is thrown out beyond itself toward what is missing: this is the first opening, the cry and the searching eye. That the seizing awakens as the first and lowest is no accident; the will must hold itself together before it can go out. Yet no one has witnessed this beginning, and the one who underwent it least of all. It is the past of every delight, a past that was never present to the will that lived it. This is why our history could not begin here. The beginning shows itself only from the end.

Hence the remarkable appearance that the last stage of our history seems to return to the first. The one who has passed through the wheel and come into releasement delights, it seems, as the child delights: without wanting, without seizing, in the mere presence of the thing. The master at the instrument and the infant at the rain wear, so to speak, the same face. This is an apparent regression, and it must be explained as one.

The difference lies here. The first stillness can be broken by any craving; the least lack awakens the wheel, and the rest is gone without remainder. The last stillness has passed through the wheel and holds both forces beneath it, the grip as its ground and the attention as its mediator. It is not broken by lack, because it has already suffered lack. The child delights in the rain until it is cold; the one who has come through can delight in the rain *and* the cold. The first stillness is a gift that is lost; the last is a gift that has been received.

The objection, therefore, does not refute the history. It only shows where the history began.

The first stillness is innocent. The last is free.

## F) General Discussion of the Doctrine of Delight Developed Here

Those who have lately spoken so much of pleasure, of reward and satisfaction, may now see what delight really is. For most of them see in it only the filling of a lack, and they have not once noticed that the deepest delight begins where nothing more is lacking and nothing more is wanted.

Plato deserves serious consideration here. Far be it from us to deny that he saw further than most who came after him. In the *Philebus* he placed pleasure among the unlimited, that which admits of more and less without end and must receive a limit from elsewhere; and perhaps no one has felt more darkly the kinship of delight with craving. But he thought pleasure chiefly as replenishment, the restoration of a disturbed harmony, and so he knew the wheel, but not the release. Aristotle came nearer when he taught that pleasure completes an activity rather than following upon it as its wage. But he knew this completion without the conflict that precedes it, as one who describes the fruit and forgets the winter.

The moderns divided what the ancients had at least held together. Kant saw, in the satisfaction we take in the beautiful, a delight without any interest \[*ein Wohlgefallen ohne alles Interesse*\], and this was a true glimpse of the will that wills nothing. But he separated it from desire as one faculty from another, and never showed how the one could become the other. The release stands in him beside the craving, not above it.

Fichte then made striving \[*Streben*\] the very essence of the I: an activity that goes out without end and is thrown back by a check it cannot remove. Here the opening will was raised into a system, and the receding horizon was declared the destiny of spirit. What did this leave of delight? Nothing but duty. An infinite striving can be honoured; it cannot be enjoyed.

Schopenhauer turned the whole against itself. For him the will is the dark ground of the world and the source of all suffering, and delight is possible only in the moments when it falls silent, when the subject becomes a pure, will-less eye before the work of art. He knew the No against the will better than anyone, and he saw the last stillness truly. But he took it for the will’s negation and not for its highest potency. Hence his delight is only a respite, a brief holiday from the servitude of willing, after which the wheel resumes.

Hegel stood nearest. He saw that craving which simply consumes its object is extinguished with it, and that labour is craving held in check \[*gehemmte Begierde*\], which preserves the thing in forming it. This is the truth of the seizing will become patient. But the labourer of Hegel delights in recognizing himself in what he has made. The will that sees itself everywhere has not yet learned to will nothing; it has only learned to seize more slowly.

And our time? It wants delight without resistance and without release. It makes every structure smooth, so that nothing need be grasped; it shows every horizon at once, so that nothing need be sought; and it supplies a new object before the old one has been either mastered or let go. The wheel is not abolished in this; it is accelerated, and its fire is made tepid. Hence the strange condition of an age that has more pleasures than any before it and complains, more than any before it, that it cannot delight.

From all of this a corollary follows for character. A person’s delight shows what kind of will he is. Whoever can delight only in possessing is held by the lowest potency; whoever can delight only in seeking has no home; whoever has never been seized by anything has no will to release. To have character is, in this sense, to have learned to let be what one has mastered.

## G) On the Other, or the Limit of All Delight

Yet the reader may not come to a standstill, even at this gain. Everything so far has concerned the will before a structure. But the will meets one thing that is not a structure, or not only one: another will.

The same succession takes place here, in a higher instance. There is a seizing of the Other: the wish to have him, which in its crudest form is domination and in its subtlest is the lover’s demand to be the whole of the beloved’s world. There is an opening toward the Other: the wish to know him entirely, to leave no corner of him unexplored. And there is the wheel, which everyone knows in its human form: possession grown indifferent, and curiosity grown restless, and the jealousy in which the two are bound together. Here too the release would be a will that wants nothing of the Other and lets him be.

But at this point something new appears, and our history must slow its pace. The Other is not merely a structure with a remainder. His remainder is himself. That in him which resists being taken is not a weight or a darkness in a thing, but personality, the act by which a being exists in a way that cannot be communicated. What in the structure was a guarantee of its reality is in the Other a freedom.

Hence the release that sufficed before the structure does not suffice here. Let one will release the Other while the Other does not release it. What appears is not delight but its counterfeit: a letting-be that has withdrawn, that wants nothing of the Other because it no longer expects anything of him. Such a will has not risen above seizing and opening; it has only ceased to be exposed. Indifference, too, wants nothing, and it is the most perfect imitation of releasement that the wheel can produce. The difference is this: the released will before a structure remains turned toward it; the indifferent will before a person has turned away, and calls its turning a freedom.

The delight in the Other therefore cannot be mine alone. A structure may be let be by one will; another will must also let itself be met. And this demands something that never arose before the structure: to be delighted in, one must consent to be a structure for another, to be grasped and to resist, to be explored and to keep a remainder. The release that was, before the structure, a resolution of the single will becomes here a double resolution, in which each lets the other be and is let be in turn. Such delight can be prepared on one side; it can be completed only on both. Whoever thinks to compel it has already returned to the wheel.

One more limit must be named. Wills that have not released one another may still build structures together, and the will can delight in such structures: in the ways of a hierarchy, the skills of an office, the competences of a system in which it finds itself only as a function. That this delight arises as we have described is true; that the structure is therefore good does not follow. Our history concerns how delight comes to be, not what deserves it. Whether a structure has closed itself against what exceeds it is a question that delight cannot answer.

This much may be said of delight. It begins in a stillness that does not know itself; it passes through a will divided into seizing and opening, and through the wheel that drives it between them, where delight is truly fulfilment and truly the exploring and handling of structures; it arrives, when it arrives, by a release that cannot be compelled, in which the will wants nothing and finds the thing whole. Before the Other, it can be received but not produced. Whether this history also holds of the delight of peoples, and of the delight that the oldest teachings ascribed to the creator in his work, it seems more fitting to leave unsaid here than to decide.

The will delights in structure only when it has ceased to want it. It delights in another only when the other, too, is free.
